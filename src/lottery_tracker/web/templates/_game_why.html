What do these five things mean?

The score out of 100 is a weighted average of the five things below. Anything at or above the keep line is worth carrying; below it, the game is doing worse for you than the space it takes up.

Win odds. How often a ticket wins anything at all. "1 in 3.17" means roughly one ticket in every three is a winner, though most of those wins are small. Pennsylvania sets this when the game is printed and it never changes for the life of the game.

Prizes left. Out of every prize this game was printed with, how many have not been claimed yet. A brand new game is near 100%. As people buy and win, it falls. A game down at 20% has had most of its good tickets taken already, so a customer buying one now has less to win.

Low-prize stock. The same idea, but counting only the small, common prizes — the five and ten dollar wins. These matter more than the jackpot for a store like yours, because they are what a regular customer actually wins, and winning a little is what brings them back tomorrow. A game can still have its million-dollar prize sitting there while every small prize is gone, and that game will feel dead at the counter.

Low-prize trend. Whether those small prizes are disappearing faster than the game as a whole. If a game is 50% sold but only 20% of its small prizes are left, the cheap wins went first and what remains is worse than the headline number suggests. We only report this when the gap is far too large to be a coincidence.

Jackpot density. This compares two things: how many of the top prizes are still unclaimed, against how much of the whole game is still unsold. If a game is half sold and half its top prizes are gone, that is normal, and it shows as 1.00 times. Above 1.00 means the big prizes have lasted longer than usual, so more of them are still out there than you would expect. Below 1.00 means they went early and the game is picked over at the top.

You will notice this one is nearly always left out of the score, and there is a good reason. Pennsylvania usually prints only five top prizes in a game, sometimes as few as three. With numbers that small, one person winning changes the figure enormously, and that tells you nothing about the game — only about one person's luck. So the number is shown to you, but it is not allowed to affect the score unless the game printed enough top prizes for the figure to mean something. In the current Pennsylvania list, only 3 games out of 67 have enough.

Where a factor says left out of the score, the remaining factors are simply reweighted between them. A missing piece of information never counts against a game.
